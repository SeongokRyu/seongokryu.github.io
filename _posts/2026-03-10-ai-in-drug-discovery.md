## Introduction

The intersection of artificial intelligence and drug discovery represents one of the most promising frontiers in modern science. Traditional drug development pipelines are notoriously expensive and time-consuming — often taking over a decade and costing billions of dollars to bring a single drug to market. AI is fundamentally reshaping this landscape.

## Key Areas Where AI Is Making an Impact

### 1. Molecular Property Prediction

Graph neural networks (GNNs) and transformer-based architectures have dramatically improved our ability to predict molecular properties such as solubility, toxicity, and binding affinity. Models like SchNet, DimeNet, and their successors leverage 3D molecular geometry to achieve remarkable accuracy.

### 2. Generative Molecular Design

Generative models — including variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and diffusion models — can now propose novel molecular structures optimized for specific therapeutic targets. This shifts the paradigm from screening existing libraries to designing molecules *de novo*.

### 3. Protein Structure Prediction

The breakthrough of AlphaFold demonstrated that AI can predict protein structures with experimental-level accuracy. This capability unlocks structure-based drug design at a scale previously unimaginable.

### 4. Retrosynthesis Planning

AI-driven retrosynthesis tools help chemists plan efficient synthetic routes for target molecules, reducing the gap between computational design and laboratory synthesis.

## Challenges Ahead

Despite remarkable progress, significant challenges remain:

- **Data scarcity**: High-quality experimental data for training models is still limited in many therapeutic areas.
- **Distribution shift**: Models trained on known chemical space may struggle to generalize to truly novel scaffolds.
- **Interpretability**: Black-box predictions are difficult to act on in a regulatory environment that demands mechanistic understanding.
- **Wet-lab validation**: Computational predictions must ultimately be validated experimentally, and the feedback loop between *in silico* and *in vitro* remains slow.

## Looking Forward

The future lies in tighter integration between AI models and experimental workflows — active learning loops where models guide experiments and experimental results continuously refine models. As foundation models for chemistry and biology mature, we can expect AI to become an indispensable partner in the quest for new therapeutics.
